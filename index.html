Revenue-neutral land value tax · 2025 secured roll · v11

What every parcel in Los Angeles, San Francisco, San Diego and Sonoma would pay if California replaced Prop 13 with a land value tax

One statewide rate on the market value of land replaces today's ad valorem property tax on land and buildings, set so California collects the same total. Land is valued with the y-intercept method on every sale of homes, offices, shops and industrial buildings, then as if there were no zoning: each spot is worth what a builder could pay for it, given what housing, office and shop space sells for there and what it costs to build at any height. By default all private land is taxed.

Why each county pays more or less

A county's land tax ÷ today's tax is the product of four factors: (statewide rate ÷ local rate today) × no-zoning uplift × market ÷ assessed land (the Prop 13 gap, plus land that's exempt today) × land's share of assessed value. Counties where land is a big share of value and assessments lag the market most pay more. Counties where value is mostly buildings, and was assessed recently, pay less.

How land is found: the y-intercept

Take every sale near a spot since 2018: houses, condos, apartments, offices, shops and warehouses. Plot what each sold for per sq ft of lot against how much building sits on each sq ft of lot (adjusted for age). Buildings add value in a straight line; the line's height at zero building is what the location itself is worth. Every 150 m cell of the map gets its own line, fitted with a smooth distance weighting, with a separate slope for homes, offices, shops and industrial.

What land value tracks

One regression across every LA parcel: the intercept (land value) against jobs within reach (every job in LA, Orange, Ventura, Riverside and San Bernardino counties, weighted by distance with a 4 km decay; Census LEHD 2023), distance to the nearest rail station (OpenStreetMap), and distance to the ocean. Testing the objections

Every change to the model is tested on prices it never saw: what builders paid for 701 development sites (the only land sold for density), FHFA's appraisal land values by tract, and vacant-land sales. With 701 sites, correlations within about ±0.02 of each other are statistically indistinguishable, and B, C and D all fall inside that band. Among ties I kept the version that measures things directly instead of extrapolating: D values new floor space at what condos and apartments actually sell for nearby. The regression slope it replaces had been extrapolating from mansions in places with no condos, such as the Los Feliz hills and Carbon Beach. Changes that did measurably worse were dropped.

From land value to a no-zoning land value

The intercept is land value under today's rules. The slopes are what one more sq ft of new floor space is worth at that spot, separately for homes, offices, shops and industrial. With no zoning, a builder could add floors until the next one costs more than it sells for. So for each height from 1 to 120 stories the model computes:

land = intercept + floor area × ( × floor value × sellable share × 80% after margin and selling costs − construction cost)

  • Uses. Upper floors are housing or offices, whichever pays more. Ground floors can be shops, and a single-story industrial or flex building is also tried. Offices and shops count only as far as the area already draws that demand, measured by its share of existing floor space that is office, retail or industrial within about 1 km. A block of houses next to Wilshire's office strip gets part of that office value, not all of it.
  • Calibration. The factor is fitted so the model reproduces what builders paid for 701 LA sites where they bought land and built 5+ units. San Francisco borrows it (21 sites) and adds 15% to construction costs. San Diego borrows it too, which makes San Diego's 232 development sites a test the model never saw: there it lands about a third above what builders paid. I did not refit it to San Diego, since that would turn the test into a fit.
  • San Diego. The SANDAG/Assessor parcel layer has no base-year field, but it records each parcel's last deed. Under Prop 13 a grant deed (a sale) resets the assessed value to the price paid, and the 2025 roll carries reassessments through December 2025 (2026 deeds aren't enrolled yet: their values sit about 25% below December's). So a sale here is a grant deed from 2018 to 2025. Quitclaims, trustee's deeds (foreclosures), timeshares, manufactured homes, Mills Act and Williamson Act parcels (assessed at restricted values), restricted-rent apartments, and buildings newer than the deed are left out. That leaves 308,000 sales. San Diego has no local rent control, so the rent-control toggle doesn't change any San Diego parcel. Two San Diego specifics: leases on public land (possessory interests: concessions on Camp Pendleton, airport hangars, marinas, golf courses on city land) carry the outline of the whole public parcel in SANDAG's layer, so they get the assessor's acreage and are taxed on their assessed leasehold land value. The public owner already collects the ground rent. Dedicated open space, conservation, mitigation and land-bank parcels (assessor codes 860–866) can't be built on and are valued like Catalina's easement land.
  • Commercial prices over time. Office, shop and industrial sales from 2018–2025 are brought to today with a price index measured from the sales themselves (each sale vs its neighbors, by year). In LA, office values are about 20% below 2018, shops 25% and industrial prices up since 2018 but down from 2022. Homes use FHFA's index.
  • New floor space is valued at what condos and apartments sell for nearby, adjusted to new construction, over a wide enough area that a few sales can't make a hot spot. Houses get their own slope in the land fit, so mansion prices no longer stand in for what dense housing would sell for.
  • Big parcels are valued like their neighbors. In the city, a golf course, studio lot or stadium parking lot could be split into ordinary lots, giving up about a quarter of the land to streets. How much to take off for size is an empirical question, so I checked what builders paid. Relative to location, the largest fifth of development sites (¾ acre to 11 acres) sold for about 4% less per sq ft than average and 11% less than mid-size sites. So a big urban parcel is now valued at the surrounding land value less up to 10%, not 25%. The steep discount for sheer acreage applies only where there's no surrounding market, like desert and mountain land.
  • Big raw parcels: valued from what acreage sells for (v11). Raw acreage sells far below the small-lot price per sq ft: buyers pay for streets, utilities, fees, entitlement time and slow absorption before land becomes lots. Every unsubdivided parcel of 1+ acres used as a ranch, farm, vineyard or vacant land is now valued from vacant-land sales of 1+ acres (2021–25), on a size curve shared by LA, San Diego and Sonoma (each county its own level) plus how nearby 5+ acre sales sit against that curve, shrunk toward the curve where few trade. Up to 2.5 acres the curve is relative to the local house-lot value (homesites trade in the house market); from 5 acres it is acreage's own price per sq ft, since remote ranch and timber land has its own market and the nearest town's lot prices say little about it. Homes on big lots keep v10.2's anchoring on top of FHFA's house-lot curve, which tested better for homes. Condo complexes and commercial or apartment sites are left out (anchoring them scrambled their ranking).
    • A data fix came first. The evidence filter dropped every land sale under $0.50/sq ft, which is every ranch selling for less than $21,800 an acre. That cut the cheap end out of the big-land evidence and biased it upward. Big vacant sales available rose from 767 to 3,533 in LA, 749 to 2,166 in San Diego and 165 to 257 in Sonoma.
    • Held-out vacant sales of 5+ acres (2 km blocks, 5 folds), share valued within 2× of the price: LA 52% → 65%, San Diego 55% → 65%, Sonoma 53% → 71%. Typical miss: 93% → 69%, 84% → 67%, 89% → 54%.
    • Sonoma's vineyards, ranches and farms, against recorded sale prices × the assessor's land share (a source the fit never sees): parcels of 20+ acres went from 4.3× to 1.5× of sale-based land, 5–20 acres from 2.6× to 1.2×; within 2×, 20% → 56% and 35% → 71%. LA's ranches went from 1.8× to 1.1×. Homes were unchanged in all three counties.
    • Census of Agriculture. USDA's 2022 census gives each county's farm acreage and the market value of farmland and buildings. Rural acreage in the model (private parcels of 10+ acres used as ranch, farm or vacant land) now averages $21k an acre in LA (Census: $22k), $20k in San Diego (Census: $36k) and $37k in Sonoma (Census: $30k). Before this fix, the model had LA at $109k, San Diego at $68k and Sonoma at $104k.
  • No-zoning premium only where dense building sells. No development-site sale in any parcel county (977 sites) has fewer than about 24,000 jobs within reach, and at the thinnest-market sites builders paid no premium over today's-rules land. Below that range the premium now fades to zero (fully gone at 24,000 jobs, full strength from the 1st-percentile site, 53,000). Without it, a few luxury second-home condo sales at The Sea Ranch, about $1,500 per sq ft, implied towers selling at that price on the remote Sonoma coast, putting Sea Ranch land at 16× its assessed value. Extrapolating past the evidence was the error. This removes about $1B of no-zoning value in San Diego's backcountry and almost none in LA. Today's-rules values there are still measured from sales.
  • No zoning on big raw land. A parcel of 5+ acres used as a house, ranch, farm or vacant land gains, with no zoning, the same proportion as the ordinary lots around it. It doesn't gain the per-sq-ft development profit across every acre. That would assume the market could absorb a town's worth of building on one ranch. Held-out tests can't see no-zoning values on raw land, so this is the conservative choice.
  • Vacant lots no longer get the house-lot size curve. FHFA's lot-size curve describes house lots. Applied to vacant land, it was discounting downtown parking lots as if they were oversized house lots. Against vacant-lot sales, dropping it raised correlation from 0.47 to 0.55 in LA and from 0.70 to 0.74 in San Diego. Houses keep the curve, where FHFA validates it.
  • Leases on public land. Port tidelands along San Diego Bay, airport and military-base leases are taxed at their assessed leasehold value. Area averages below leave them out.
  • Vacant hillside land is valued at what vacant land actually sells for nearby, not at the value of the built lots around it. The parcels still vacant in the hills are mostly the steep, landlocked or unserved ones, and they sell accordingly: about $44/sq ft in the Hollywood Hills vs $300+ for lots with houses.
  • Conservation easements. About 40,000 acres of Catalina belong to the Santa Catalina Island Conservancy under a perpetual 1974 open-space easement held by LA County. That land can't be developed, so it's valued at its permitted-use (grazing/recreation) value, about $500 an acre.
  • Lot size, tested and rejected. I tried measuring house land per sq ft on a standard 7,500 sq ft lot, so that big-lot areas like Point Dume would be valued as if their lots could be split. It made the fit to builders' prices, FHFA and vacant sales worse, so it's off. In the cities, big parcels still get no per-parcel size discount in the no-zoning value.
  • Rent control toggle. With rent control kept, each covered building's land is worth less by what the cap takes off its sale price (the gap between the rent-controlled and ordinary apartment slopes, times its floor area). Those parcels pay less, and the statewide rate rises to make up the difference. That's about $59B of land value in LA and $27B in SF. Other counties' rent-controlled stock isn't in the parcel data, so it isn't included.
  • Rent control. Apartments old enough to fall under rent control (pre-1979 in LA City, Santa Monica, West Hollywood and San Francisco; pre-1995 in Beverly Hills, Culver City, Inglewood and Pasadena) sell with tenants whose rents are capped. That discount belongs to the building's regulation, not the land, so those sales get their own slope and don't drag down the intercept. Every parcel, rent-controlled or not, is valued as if unencumbered, which is what abolishing rent control would make it. This raised land values in Koreatown, Mid-City and Culver City by 5–10% and improved the fit to what builders paid.
  • Construction quality, tested and rejected. I tested whether luxury construction was being counted as land by anchoring each area's building value to the assessor's valuation of new homes built there (twice as high per sq ft in Manhattan Beach as in East Hollywood). It made every held-out check worse, so it's off. FHFA's appraisal land values, which do subtract quality-adjusted construction cost, rank Manhattan Beach above the Palisades and Hancock Park above Koreatown's house blocks too.
  • Lot size. Land under houses gets FHFA's lot-size curve relative to the typical lot sold nearby; apartment, commercial and development land does not.
  • Hillsides. Where vacant land sells far below the intercept, the building part of the value is discounted by up to 60%.

What this cannot tell you

  • The intercept still reflects today's rules: a lot where apartments are legal sells for more than one where they aren't. The no-zoning step adds what the rules forbid. It never subtracts what they protect, such as the scarcity premium of low-density beach towns.
  • Each parcel is valued as if it alone were rebuilt. If zoning disappeared everywhere at once, supply would rise and floor values would fall.
  • Offices are priced at 2024–25 sale prices, which are deeply depressed, especially in San Francisco (about $300 per sq ft of building). That's why office towers rarely pay, even downtown.
  • The other 54 counties carry the most uncertainty (see below), and with it the statewide rate: 0.86% with no zoning, plausibly 0.66–0.96%.
  • Land under homes runs 25% above FHFA in LA and San Diego and 43% above in San Francisco. FHFA's appraisal-based values run low in expensive markets, but part of the gap may be ours.

The other 54 counties

Each county's land is estimated from four pieces, calibrated on the parcel counties:

  • House land: FHFA single-family land values × DOF home counts.
  • Apartment and condo land: land per unit relative to land per house, measured on parcels.
  • Non-residential land: BOE assessed land × the market-to-assessed ratio, scaled by each county's Prop 13 gap.
  • No-zoning uplift: read from FHFA tract prices with the uplift-by-price curve measured on parcels.

Southern and Central California counties use LA's and San Diego's ratios. Bay Area counties use all four parcel counties. Both rules were compared with the alternatives (everyone uses all four; Sonoma joins LA and San Diego for non-Bay counties) on the test below, and this one missed least.

The test is to predict each parcel county from the others under that same rule. Under today's rules it misses LA by −21%, SF by +27%, San Diego by +20% and Sonoma by −32%. With no zoning it misses by −9%, +22%, +19% and −39%. The typical misses (24% and 20%) are the whiskers on the county chart. Together with calibrating on each parcel county alone, they put the statewide rate at 0.86% with no zoning, plausibly 0.66–0.96%. The slider scales all 54 estimates together.

What Sonoma taught. It's the first parcel county that is mostly suburban and rural, and LA and San Diego's ratios underpredict it by about a third. Sonoma's land under homes runs 1.48× FHFA's values, against 0.63–0.81× in the other three. That one county is why the low end of the rate range is now 0.66%: calibrating everything on Sonoma alone would value the state's land about 30% higher. Sonoma's own level may run high, too. Its intercept sits 2.25× above what builders paid on 21 development sites (many of them post-fire rebuilds) and 53% above FHFA, against 23–26% in LA and San Diego. Its small vacant lots, though, sit as close to their sale prices as LA's (1.20× vs 1.23×). So the county estimates for the North Bay and the farm counties carry real uncertainty, and the whiskers show it.

Farmland, tested and not adopted. Farmland under Williamson Act contracts is assessed at restricted values, so the commercial market-to-assessed ratio could understate it. I tried replacing it with the Census of Agriculture's farm values. That improved San Diego's prediction (+20% → +17%) but worsened Sonoma's (−32% → −39%), because rural acreage per dollar of census farm value varies too much between counties (LA's is mostly non-farm desert and mountain land). The Census still serves as a check on the parcel model's rural values (above).

No-zoning uplift from tract features, re-tested with four counties and not adopted. Each county's uplift is predicted either from the uplift-by-price curve or from a regression on FHFA tract features. The regression predicted Sonoma and San Diego's uplift a little better but LA and SF's worse, and its no-zoning test misses rose from 20% to 27%. The curve stays.

Orange's tax map gives every parcel's assessed land, building value and tax bill, but no sale dates or building sizes, so it can't run the parcel model.

Taxes

Today's tax is each county's average rate (1% plus voter debt) on net assessed value, including today's $7,000 homeowners' exemption. The toggles decide whether the land tax keeps that exemption and whether nonprofit, church and university land pays. Government land stays exempt.
